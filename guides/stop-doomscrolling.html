How to stop doomscrolling

Last updated November 2026

It is not a willpower problem, and treating it as one is why the same resolution gets made every Sunday. The feed removed the moment you would have stopped at; the fix is to put an edge back.

What it actually is

Doomscrolling is the compulsive consumption of bad news — a feed of it, at speed, past the point of being informed and into the point of being agitated. The word arrived around 2020 and the behaviour is older than the word: threat monitoring is one of the things attention does automatically, because missing a danger used to be more expensive than noticing a hundred false alarms.

A feed is the perfect delivery system for that instinct. Novelty keeps you reading, negativity keeps you alert, and the end — the moment where you would normally stop — never arrives.

Why willpower is not on the list

You are not trying to resist a single impulse. You are trying to resist a stream of them, each one fresh and each one loaded before you decided you wanted it. Nobody wins that by intending to more firmly. The protocol below works on the environment instead, because the environment is what keeps winning.

1. Remove the trigger from the reflex layer

The dock and the first Home Screen are the reflex layer: the places your thumb goes without a decision. Anything there is opened far more often than the same app buried two screens away.

  • Take the news and social apps off the first screen and out of the dock.
  • Turn off their notifications. A badge that says "12" is a trigger with a number on it.
  • If one app is the problem, delete it and use the web version for a week. This is the option people skip and the one with the largest immediate effect.

2. Put a paced limit on the two apps that do it

Not a daily budget — the thing doomscrolling is made of is long uninterrupted sessions, and a daily budget does not interrupt anything. A five-minute allowance every hour does: the session ends while you are in it, and the shield tells you when the next one is.

In Screen Limiter: +, pick the app, 5 minutes, one-hour window, Normal. Two apps is the right number to start with. Ten is a project you will abandon.

3. Make bedtime a boundary, not a decision

Most doomscrolling happens in bed, and in bed the cost of continuing is invisible until the morning. Two changes make the decision unnecessary:

  • Charge the phone somewhere that is not the bedroom. If it is the alarm, replace it with an alarm clock.
  • If it must stay, put a 30-minute window on the news and social groups after 21:30, so the last session of the day is short by design.

4. Run it for a week and look at the data

Seven days is long enough to see whether the change did anything and short enough that you will actually finish it. Screen Limiter's Insights show how many windows you stayed under per group, which is a better measure than a total — the total can improve while the evenings get worse.

Judge it on three questions: did I go to bed earlier, was the first hour of the day calmer, and did I miss anything I actually needed? The third one is almost always no.

What to do instead, specifically

"Just stop" leaves a hole where the habit was, and a hole gets filled by the same habit. Name the replacement in advance, and make it something within reach: a book on the table by the sofa, a podcast queued, a shower, a person in the next room. The replacement does not need to be virtuous. It needs to be easier than finding the app.

When it is anxiety rather than a habit

If the need to check is driven by a genuine fear that something will happen while you are not looking, blocking the app treats the symptom and leaves the fear in place. The better version of that arrangement is a scheduled check — one 10-minute window at a fixed time, deliberately, with the rest of the day off the hook — and, if the fear is persistent and intrusive, a conversation with someone qualified.

Questions

Is doomscrolling an addiction?

The word is used loosely and clinically it means something specific. Problematic use of feeds is real and studied; "addiction" as a diagnosis for phone use is not settled. The practical difference matters less than you would think: the interventions that work are environmental either way.

Does grayscale help?

Some people find it makes the phone less attractive, and small studies support a modest effect. It is free to try: Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Color Filters → Grayscale, with a triple-click shortcut.

How long does it take for the pull to fade?

Most people report the reflex weakening within a week or two, with the first three days being the worst. If it has not faded after a month, the trigger is probably still there — go back to step 1.
