What short sessions do to attention

Last updated November 2026

You have probably read that human attention spans have fallen to eight seconds. That number has no study behind it. What is real is more specific, and more fixable.

The eight-second attention span is not real

You have seen it: the human attention span has fallen to eight seconds, shorter than a goldfish. It appears in conference slides, in adverts, in the opening paragraph of a hundred articles about phones.

Trace it back and it ends in a 2015 marketing report from Microsoft Canada, which cites a statistics aggregator rather than a study. There is no primary research behind it, and the sources it gestures at are not measuring attention span at all — they measure things like how long people look at a page before clicking away, which is a fact about web pages, not about human beings.

This matters because the claim is used to sell solutions to a problem it invented. If your attention span were eight seconds, no habit change would help. It is worth being precise about what actually does go wrong.

What goes wrong is switching, not shrinking

When you move from one task to another, part of your attention does not move with you. Sophie Leroy called this attention residue: performance on the new task drops because the previous one is still occupying working memory, and the effect is strongest when the first task was left unfinished or you are under time pressure.

A feed is a machine for generating unfinished business. Each item opens a small loop — a thought, a comparison, a thing you meant to look up — and the next item interrupts before any of them close. You do not come back from five minutes of scrolling; you come back from five minutes of scrolling with a handful of unresolved loops and a slightly worse ability to start the thing you actually sat down to do.

Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168–181.

The design removes the stopping cue

Three things make a feed unusually hard to leave, and none of them are mysterious:

  • Variable reward. Some items are excellent and most are forgettable, on no schedule you can predict. Unpredictable reward is the pattern that produces the most persistent checking behaviour — the same reason a slot machine is more compelling than a vending machine that always returns a known snack.
  • Infinite scroll. There is no page bottom, no "load more" pause, no moment where the content announces its end.
  • Autoplay. The next thing starts before you have decided to want it. The decision has been taken out of the loop.

None of this requires a conspiracy, and the people who built it are subject to the same design. It requires only that the cheapest action be continuing.

Why "just use it less" does not work

Intentions are a poor predictor of behaviour in the moment they are tested. The gap between what people plan to do and what they do with a phone is one of the most replicated findings in the area, and it is not a character flaw — it is what happens when a considered intention meets an immediate cue with no cost.

Friction flips the default. If opening the app takes a decision, a hold and four extra taps, a meaningful share of the automatic opens simply do not happen. The phone time that disappears is not the time you wanted; it is the time you were not really present for.

What five minutes an hour changes — and what it doesn't

It does not lengthen your attention span. Nothing in an app can do that. What it changes is the structure around the habit:

  • It caps the tail. Five minutes rarely becomes ninety. The session has a container instead of a fence somewhere in the distance.
  • It supplies the stopping cue the feed removed. A shield with a time on it is a hard edge, and hard edges are what make a break feel like a break rather than a decision you are still making.
  • It reduces the number of switches. One considered return an hour instead of eleven unplanned ones is a real reduction in residue, even if the total minutes were the same.

What it cannot do is give you back the deep work you have not started. That needs a block of time, a single task, and a phone in another room. The limit protects the edges of that block; it does not fill it.

If you want to test this on yourself

  1. Pick the one app you open without deciding to. Not the worst one — the most automatic one.
  2. Put it on a five-minute allowance, hourly, for a week.
  3. Keep a note of what you do in the minute after the shield appears. That minute is the honest measure of what the scrolling was for.

Questions

Does TikTok or Instagram shorten your attention span?

There is no good evidence that it does. There is evidence that short-form use is associated with self-reported attention difficulties, and that switching tasks leaves residue that harms the next task. "Shorter attention span" is the version that fits a headline; "more interrupted attention" is the version the data supports.

Is attention residue permanent?

No. It is a short-term cost of switching, and it fades when the previous task is completed or written down. The note you make before switching is doing real work: it closes the loop enough for the residue to shrink.

Would a five-minute limit make me less productive?

It makes you less available, which is the point. Most people find the minutes it removes were not serving the work; the ones that were, you notice immediately and adjust.
