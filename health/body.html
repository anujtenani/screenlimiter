Eyes, neck and hands

Last updated November 2026

The physical cost of a long session is real but rarely dramatic: dry eyes, a dull headache, a stiff neck. The fix is frequency, not duration.

Digital eye strain is real, common and temporary

Staring at a screen for a long stretch produces a predictable set of symptoms: dry or gritty eyes, a dull ache behind them, blurred vision that clears when you look away, a headache that starts at the end of the day. The American Optometric Association calls the cluster computer vision syndrome, and its causes are unglamorous — reduced blinking, sustained near focus, a screen slightly too close or too high.

None of that is damage. The discomfort is from muscles held in one position and a tear film that is not being replenished, which is why it resolves with rest and returns with the next long session.

American Optometric Association — digital eyestrain

The blink rate is the mechanism

People blink far less when looking at a screen than when looking at anything else, and the blinks they do make are more likely to be incomplete — the lid does not quite reach the bottom, so the tear film is not fully renewed. A dry surface is an uncomfortable surface, and an uncomfortable surface makes you blink more awkwardly, not better.

The 20-20-20 rule exists because it interrupts the pattern: every twenty minutes, look at something twenty feet away for twenty seconds. It sounds too simple to matter. It works because it changes the two variables the discomfort is made of — blink rate and focal distance.

Holding the phone closer than you think

The average viewing distance for a phone is much shorter than for a desktop monitor, which means more convergence and more accommodation for the same amount of reading. Holding it lower, rather than closer, gives the eyes a longer distance without asking you to hold it at arm's length for an hour.

"Tech neck" is a load problem, not a posture crime

The head weighs a good deal more than people assume, and tipping it forward multiplies the load the neck has to carry. Twenty minutes in that position is not an injury; four hours in it, most days, is a slow accumulation of muscular tension that shows up as a headache and a stiff shoulder blade long before anything dramatic.

The intervention is not a perfect posture you have to maintain. It is variation — being in a different position often, even a worse one, beats holding a good one until it hurts.

Hands and wrists

Thumb scrolling is a repetitive motion in an awkward range, and the tendons at the base of the thumb are not built for hours of it. Pain at the wrist or thumb side, worse when gripping, is the common presentation and it is worth taking seriously early rather than working through.

Why short sessions beat one long break

A single break after two hours does not undo two hours of held position. Frequent small interruptions do, because the tissue never gets far enough into the bad position to complain. This is the same argument that applies to sleep and attention, which is convenient: one design decision, several benefits.

What the window gives you for free

A five-minute allowance on an hourly window ends the session automatically, whether or not you were going to take a break. You look up, you look somewhere else, you put the phone down. It is a 20-20-20 rule that enforces itself.

When to see someone

  • Vision that stays blurred after you stop looking at a screen.
  • Pain that persists for days, or numbness and tingling in the fingers.
  • Headaches that are new, worsening, or wake you.
  • Eye pain, redness or light sensitivity rather than dryness and ache.

An optometrist can check the boring explanations — refraction, dry eye, convergence — that get blamed on screens and are cheap to fix.

Questions

Does screen time damage your eyes permanently?

There is no good evidence that it does. Eye strain is a temporary discomfort, not damage. The one genuinely contested area is myopia in children, where more time outdoors is consistently associated with slower progression — a reason to go outside, not a reason to fear a phone.

Do blue-light filters help?

For comfort, a little, for some people. For sleep, the evidence is weaker than the marketing. For eye strain specifically, the research does not show a meaningful benefit beyond the reduction in brightness. Taking breaks does more.

Does holding my phone lower help?

Yes, modestly. A phone held lower and slightly further away reduces convergence and neck flexion at the same time. It is not a fix for a two-hour session; it makes the session less bad.
