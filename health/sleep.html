Screens, sleep and the hour before bed

Last updated November 2026

The last hour of scrolling is the most expensive one — not because blue light is poison, but because it keeps postponing the decision you were about to make.

What the light actually does

The best-known experiment on this is small but unusually clean. Twelve healthy adults spent five nights reading either a light-emitting e-reader or a printed book before bed. On the e-reader nights, melatonin release was delayed by more than an hour, the body clock shifted later, people felt less sleepy in the evening, and they were less alert the next morning after the same amount of sleep.

That is a real effect, and it is worth being precise about its size: it is a shift, not a knockout. Light does not stop you sleeping. It moves the point at which your body expected to start trying.

Chang et al., PNAS 2015

What the feed does to bedtime

Light is only half of it. The larger effect is behavioural, and it has a name: bedtime procrastination — voluntarily delaying going to bed despite knowing it will cost you, because the evening is the only part of the day that feels like yours.

Kroese and colleagues described it as a failure of self-regulation rather than a scheduling problem, and that framing matters. You are not bad at time management at 11pm. You are tired, which is precisely when the part of you that plans and the part of you that wants something pleasant stop agreeing.

Kroese et al., Frontiers in Psychology 2014

The video that never ends

A video has a natural end. A feed has engineered the natural end away: the next item is already playing, or already loaded, or the algorithm has queued four more. There is no moment at which you are asked to decide to continue, so the decision to stop has to be made from scratch, against a stream of things designed to be worth one more.

This is why "one more video" is a joke about a failure everyone recognises. The failure is not weak character. It is a missing stopping cue.

Why "phone off at 11" fails

A fixed bedtime is a cliff. At 10:55 you are halfway through something, and the rule asks you to abandon it mid-episode — so you don't. You finish it. Then the next one starts. The rule was designed as a wall but experienced as a suggestion, and it takes one exception to retire it.

A window is different in one specific way: it arrives while you are between things more often than while you are inside one. Five minutes an hour means the block lands at twenty past, not at the exact moment the plot resolves. And because the next window is only an hour away, going to bed instead costs you nothing you can talk yourself into resenting.

A wind-down that survives a real evening

  1. Charge the phone outside the bedroom. If it is your alarm, buy a $10 alarm clock. This single change does more than every app in this category.
  2. Make the evening group the smallest of the day. A separate group, active from 21:00 to 23:00, with five minutes and a half-hour window. The restriction applies where it is needed instead of everywhere, which is why you will keep it.
  3. Give the minutes somewhere to go. A limit that removes a thing without naming a replacement becomes a limit you argue with. Put a book, a shower or a person in the slot.
  4. Take the first hour of the morning outdoors. Morning light is the other end of the same lever: it advances the clock that the evening light pushed back.

What matters less than you have been told

  • Blue-light glasses. The evidence for a meaningful sleep benefit in real use is weak and inconsistent. Night Shift is a small nudge, not a fix, and neither replaces the behavioural change.
  • The exact colour temperature of your screen. Warmer is marginally better. Scrolling until 1am with a warm screen is still scrolling until 1am.
  • The perfect routine. The correlation between complicated wind-down rituals and actually going to bed on time is not impressive. Consistency beats sophistication.

Where this app stops

Screen Limiter can make the last window of the day small and put a shield on the app at 22:40. It cannot make you put the phone down, and it cannot give you back the sleep you have already lost. If you are waking repeatedly, snoring heavily or sleeping badly for weeks despite a reasonable routine, that is a conversation with a doctor rather than a settings problem.

Questions

Does screen time before bed really affect sleep?

The light effect is real and measurable: melatonin is suppressed and delayed, and the body clock shifts later. The behavioural effect — staying up later because the content does not end — is usually larger. Both point at the same fix: stop earlier, not brighter.

Is it the light or the content?

Both, and the content usually wins. A calm e-reader is not the same as an autoplaying feed of short videos. If you want a single change, move the feed out of the last hour and keep the reading.

How long before bed should I stop?

The common recommendation is an hour, which is a compromise between how long melatonin suppression lasts and how long most people are willing to give up. Thirty minutes is better than nothing; two hours is better than thirty. The number matters less than it happening at the same time every night.
