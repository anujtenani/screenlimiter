Why it matters

Last updated November 2026

Screen time is not automatically a health problem and it is not a moral failing. The evidence is narrower, and more useful, than either the headlines or the marketing suggests.

The short version

Three claims survive contact with the research, and one popular one does not:

  • Heavy use is associated with worse sleep. The association is consistent across studies, and the mechanism is partly behavioural: the session delays bedtime, not just melatonin.
  • For adolescents, more social media use predicts more depressive symptoms over time. The effect is small at population scale and largest at the heavy end, which is exactly where most people who download an app like this are.
  • Attention is interrupted more than it is shortened. The eight-second attention span you have seen quoted has no study behind it; the measurable cost is what a switch does to the task you return to.
  • There is no evidence that screen time on its own causes permanent eye damage. Eye strain is real, common and temporary. The permanent-risk claims outrun the data.

The shape of the problem is the interesting part

Total screen time is the number everyone measures, and it is the least actionable one. "Less" is not a plan. The things the research keeps pointing at are more specific than a total:

The session that never gets interrupted

A feed has no natural end. There is no credits roll, no page to close, no point at which the content says "that is enough". Every continuation is a fresh decision that the design has already made cheaper than stopping — the next item is loaded before you know you wanted it. This is why "just put it down" is such common advice and such useless advice.

The hour you meant to protect

Most people do not want less of everything. They want to stop losing the hour before bed, or the first hour of the morning, or the minutes between finishing work and starting dinner. A daily budget is a poor fit for that want, because it can be spent in the least important hour of the day and leaves you unprotected in the most important one. That is the argument on why daily limits fail, and it is the reason this app paces time instead of rationing it once.

Proximity and friction

The most reliable finding in the behavioural literature is also the dullest: convenience wins. A phone within reach is used more than the same phone across the room; an app one tap away is opened more than the same app four taps away. Nothing in a blocker treats the underlying habit. What it can do is make the automatic version of the behaviour stop working, so that the decision has to be made consciously and takes a few seconds longer than the impulse lasts.

What Screen Limiter does about it

Modest, specific things:

  • It puts a stopping cue where the feed does not have one: a shield that names the exact time the minutes come back.
  • It keeps the cost of a bad moment to one window rather than a whole day, which is what makes such a limit survivable for months instead of a week.
  • It gives you a week of outcomes — windows stayed under, windows missed — without sending any of it anywhere.

It does not treat anxiety, dependency or a sleep disorder, and it is not presented as if it does. There is no streak to protect and no score that implies health. It is a small piece of friction, chosen once, that repeats.

Questions

Is screen time bad for you?

Heavy use is associated with worse sleep and, in adolescents, more depressive symptoms. The effect sizes in population studies are small and causality is contested. "Less screen time" is not automatically healthier — the sleep you lose, the movement you skip, the people you are not with are what matter.

Does reducing screen time improve wellbeing?

Some trials find modest improvements when people cut social media use, particularly when the reduction is enough to change sleep. The improvements tend to be small and uneven. A limit that you keep for a year beats a reduction you abandon in a fortnight.

Who is this written for?

Adults who want to stop losing specific hours. The evidence about adolescents is included where it is relevant, but Screen Limiter is not a parental-control product — for a child's phone, Apple's Screen Time with a passcode is the right tool, and no third-party app can match it.

General information, not medical advice. This page summarises published research so you can judge whether a limit is worth the friction. It is not a diagnosis and it is not a treatment. If sleep, mood, attention or pain is interfering with your life, talk to a clinician — no app is a substitute for that conversation.
