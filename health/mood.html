Social media, comparison and mood

Last updated November 2026

The honest version: the associations are real, the effect sizes are small, and the group most at risk is the one least able to opt out.

Start with the size of the effect. Population studies find a small negative association between heavy social media use and wellbeing. Small means small: at the scale of thousands of people, it explains well under one percent of the variation in how they feel. For a heavy user with a vulnerable history, that average can still be a bad deal — but it is not a public health emergency, and it is not a diagnosis.

The study everyone cites

The most-quoted longitudinal study followed 3,826 Canadian adolescents for several years, measuring social media use and depressive symptoms. Each additional hour of daily social media use was associated with a small increase in reported depressive symptoms, and the association was strongest among the heaviest users. The relationship was not explained away by the obvious confounders the authors could measure.

Boers et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2019

The study that complicates it

A much larger analysis of 355,358 adolescents found the same direction of effect and a very different magnitude. The association between digital technology use and wellbeing was negative, statistically robust and tiny — about as large as the association with eating potatoes or wearing glasses. The authors argued that the effect is real but too small to justify the attention it receives as a population-level explanation.

Orben & Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour 2019

Both are good studies. They disagree about magnitude, not direction, and the disagreement is mostly about what question is being asked: "does this shift the average?" (barely) versus "does this harm a vulnerable subset?" (sometimes, substantially).

Why the headlines keep flipping

  • Measurement. Self-reported screen time correlates poorly with actual logged usage. A study built on estimates is describing a different quantity than one built on device logs.
  • Lumping. "Screen time" includes reading, video calls with grandparents and a spreadsheet. Averaging those into one number hides the thing being argued about.
  • Selection. Adolescents who are already depressed use more social media, and studies that do not follow people over time cannot tell which came first.
  • Incentives. Both "phones are destroying a generation" and "the effect is trivial" are easier to publish than "it depends, and mostly on what you are doing".

Comparison is the part with the clearest mechanism

Social comparison is not new — Festinger described it in 1954 — but a feed changes its supply. You no longer compare yourself to the people around you; you compare yourself to a curated highlight reel from everyone, selected for engagement by a system that has no interest in whether the comparison is fair.

Upward comparison is the reliable part: seeing people who appear to be doing better than you tends to lower mood, and the effect is strongest when the comparison feels close and relevant. A feed specialises in making strangers feel close and relevant.

Meta's own research

In September 2021, the Wall Street Journal published internal Facebook research on Instagram's effect on teenage girls. The slides described body-image harm for a meaningful share of users, and internal researchers proposed changes that were not adopted. Meta disputed parts of the characterisation and has since added teen defaults — but the episode is a useful corrective to the idea that the only disagreement is between outside critics and the company.

What helps that is not "delete the app"

  1. Cap the session, not the day. The comparison damage happens inside the session, and a session with an end is a different experience from one with none.
  2. Curate aggressively. Mute accounts that reliably make you feel worse. It feels petty for one afternoon and pays for years.
  3. Keep the phone out of the bedroom. The comparison loops that run at midnight are the ones that linger.
  4. Run a week, then look. Screen Limiter's Insights count windows and outcomes rather than app identities; the useful question is whether the evening got easier, not whether the number got smaller.

When this is not a screen problem

If you are depressed, anxious, or not sleeping for weeks, an app limit is not the first thing to try. Neither social media nor its absence treats a mood disorder. The useful version of the claim is narrow: for some people, at some doses, in some contexts, the feed makes an existing difficulty worse, and reducing it is one small lever among several. Talk to a clinician about the others.

Questions

Is social media bad for mental health?

Heavy use is associated with more depressive symptoms in adolescents, with small average effects and larger ones for heavy users. Causality is contested and the direction can run both ways. "Bad for mental health" is a stronger claim than the evidence supports; "not neutral, especially at high doses, especially for teenagers" is defensible.

Would quitting social media make me happier?

Some trials find modest improvements after a few weeks of reduced use, most clearly when sleep improves. Others find nothing. If you are going to test it, make it a defined experiment with a defined end, and judge it on sleep and mood rather than on how productive you felt.

Does Screen Limiter measure my mood?

No. It stores seven days of window outcomes, on the device. There is no mood tracking, no journaling and no score. If you want to know whether something changed, you have to notice it.
